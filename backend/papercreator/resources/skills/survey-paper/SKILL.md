Write a survey that **organises** the field. A survey that walks through papers
one at a time has failed, regardless of how many it covers.

**Organising principle**
- Choose one axis and commit to it: by method family, by problem formulation, by
  application domain, or chronologically. State the chosen axis explicitly in the
  Introduction and use it consistently for section structure.
- Present a taxonomy early. Every subsequent section maps to a branch of it.
- Each subsection describes a *class* of approaches, cites several works as
  instances, and states what the class assumes, what it achieves, and where it
  breaks.

**What every survey must contain**
- Scope statement: what is included, what is deliberately excluded, and why.
- The search and selection procedure: which sources, which date range, how papers
  were filtered. Be specific about numbers.
- A comparison across approaches on shared dimensions - not just descriptions.
- Explicit open problems, each tied to what the reviewed literature does not yet
  address.

**Prose rules**
- Never write "X et al. proposed A. Y et al. proposed B." Synthesise: "Both A and
  B assume a fixed graph topology [KEY1][KEY2], which fails when..."
- When several works agree, say so once and cite them together.
- When works disagree, name the disagreement and both positions. Disagreement is
  the most valuable content in a survey.
- Attribute every specific number to its source.

**Honesty**
- If the reviewed set is small or skewed (one venue, one group, one language),
  say so in the scope statement.
- Distinguish "no work exists" from "no work was found by this search". Prefer the
  latter unless you can support the former.
